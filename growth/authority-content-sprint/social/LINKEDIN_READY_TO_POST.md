## P1 — Pablo | The career is there. The record is scattered. | Week 1
A career can be impressive and surprisingly hard to understand online.

I keep seeing variations of the same pattern while researching established professionals:

A biography on an employer's website.
Publications in one database.
Speaking engagements somewhere else.
A LinkedIn profile that captures the timeline, but not the work.

None of those sources is necessarily wrong. Together, they can still leave a stranger with an incomplete picture.

And that matters most when the person evaluating you doesn't already know you.

An independent website isn't a shortcut to authority. It's a way to organize authority that already exists.

I think that's a more useful conversation than “everyone needs a personal brand.”

For people with long, complex careers: where do you think the clearest record of your work lives today?

## J1 — Juanita | A professional website as an editorial problem | Week 1
Something I find interesting about experienced professionals: often the most important information about their work already exists publicly.

It's just scattered.

A talk is on a conference page. Research is in a database. A current role is on LinkedIn. An older achievement lives on a former employer's website.

The challenge isn't necessarily writing a more impressive biography.

It's deciding what someone needs to understand first, what evidence supports it, and how to make the rest easy to explore.

That editorial decision can make a professional website much more useful than another long résumé.

Question for anyone who has had to evaluate a specialist online: what information do you wish was easier to find?

## P2 — Pablo | Your website is not your acquisition strategy | Week 1
One uncomfortable truth about professional websites:

A better website won't fix a business that isn't reaching buyers.

If an independent consultant has no relevant conversations, the problem might be positioning, referrals, distribution or sales.

A website can help after someone becomes curious: it can give them a clear account of what you do, why it matters, and what is verifiable.

But confusing *credibility infrastructure* with *demand generation* is a very expensive mistake.

We build the first. We think carefully about how it connects to the second.

Does your website currently help people evaluate you—or are you expecting it to do all the selling?

## P3 — Pablo | The expert witness test | Week 2
Imagine an attorney trying to evaluate a technical expert.

They probably don't need a more dramatic tagline.

They need to know:
— What does this person actually specialize in?
— Which claims can be verified?
— Where are the publications or public technical records?
— What is the right way to get in touch?

If it takes ten tabs to answer those questions, there may be an information design problem.

I'm increasingly interested in websites as *professional records* rather than digital brochures.

Obviously, client confidentiality and factual accuracy come before presentation.

For people who evaluate experts: what is the first thing you look for?

## J2 — Juanita | Less biography, more evidence | Week 2
“30+ years of experience” tells me time has passed.

It doesn't tell me which problem someone knows how to solve.

For a senior professional, a great website can show the specific areas of work, the progression behind them, and selected sources that substantiate the story.

The goal isn't to make the person sound important.

It's to reduce the work a reader has to do to understand why their experience might be relevant.

What is the one thing missing from most professional bios you read?

## P4 — Pablo | Expertise ≠ visibility | Week 2
A person can be highly respected inside a profession and barely legible outside it.

That doesn't mean they lack authority.
It means much of that authority lives in private context: referrals, colleagues, institutions and years of accumulated work.

When they go independent, those shortcuts disappear for new buyers.

The useful question becomes:
*What would someone need to see to understand this work without an introduction?*

A website is only one possible answer. The thinking has to come first.

## P5 — Pablo | Leaving a corporate career | Week 3
A senior title explains your place in a company.

It rarely explains what someone should hire you to do independently.

That's a difficult transition.

After decades inside one organization, the work may be substantial—but the positioning can still be unclear.

A simple exercise for anyone going independent:
1. Who has the problem you solve?
2. What triggers them to seek help?
3. What kind of engagement can you actually deliver?
4. What evidence makes your expertise credible?

Only then should you worry about the website.

What's been the hardest part of moving from a company title to an independent offer?

## J3 — Juanita | Your past employer is not your whole professional identity | Week 3
Imagine someone spent 20 years doing meaningful work inside one organization.

The organization's website might document their last role very well.

But it may not tell the story of the broader person: earlier work, published ideas, cross-disciplinary experience, or what they're doing today.

When people become independent, that gap can suddenly become visible.

I like the idea of a digital home that belongs to the professional, with links back to the original sources—rather than replacing them.

Has anyone here had to rebuild how they introduce themselves after leaving a long-term role?

## P6 — Pablo | Test the need before the site | Week 3
Not everyone needs a personal website.

If your LinkedIn and current professional profile answer every question your clients ask, you might already be covered.

If your best work is scattered across papers, patents, institutions, interviews and previous employers, a dedicated site can be valuable.

If your problem is simply that no one knows you exist, you may need distribution before design.

The right solution starts with the right diagnosis.

That's also a useful filter for deciding who *not* to sell to.
