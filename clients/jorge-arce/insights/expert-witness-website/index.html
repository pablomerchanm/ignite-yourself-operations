What Should an Expert Witness Website Include?

A practical checklist for expert witnesses: how to present expertise, credentials, publications, appropriate public testimony, and contact information responsibly.

By Ignite YourselfOctober 20, 20264 min read

General information, not legal advice. Requirements differ by jurisdiction and discipline.

When an attorney or research assistant evaluates a potential expert, they are not simply looking for a striking photograph or a polished tagline.

They need to understand the person's qualifications, area of expertise, relevant work and whether that professional may fit the matter.

An independent expert website should help a reader make an informed first assessment. It should not overstate qualifications or expose confidential litigation information.

Start with the question counsel is actually asking

Instead of opening with “world-renowned expert,” consider a more useful introduction:

  • What is the expert's primary discipline?
  • Which issues are within—and outside—their scope?
  • What kinds of professional or technical work support their knowledge?
  • What documents can verify selected qualifications?
  • How should appropriate enquiries be directed?

A clear website is an aid to evaluation, not an alternative to legal vetting, a curriculum vitae, or required expert disclosures.

1. A precise professional introduction

Include full name, relevant professional titles where verified, principal practice area, location or jurisdiction where pertinent, and a concise statement of services offered.

Avoid claims such as “the leading expert” unless substantiated and actually useful. Accuracy matters more than adjective intensity.

2. Areas of expertise organized by questions

Do not simply present every subject the expert has ever encountered. Group experience into meaningful specialties that correspond to real enquiries.

For example, an economist might distinguish earning-capacity analysis, economic damages and valuation—only where supported by their actual background.

A technical consultant might organize work by materials, processes or applications. Plain-language headings help general counsel or a legal assistant navigate even when the underlying discipline is complex.

3. Education, qualifications and appointments

List relevant degrees, credentials, licenses and affiliations with dates and sources where appropriate. Clarify whether a designation remains current. Avoid using institutional logos without permission or implying an endorsement by a former employer.

4. Publications, patents and selected public work

For research-active specialists, a curated list of publications and patents can be more helpful than a generic paragraph about “thought leadership.”

For each item, use a reliable citation, title, year, author details and a link to a publisher, patent database or other authoritative public record when available. Remove duplicates. Make it clear when full text is restricted.

5. Testimony and case information—with boundaries

Where professionally appropriate and legally permissible, an expert may provide a selected account of publicly documented engagements or experience. Do not publish protected work product, sealed matters, private client details, privileged material or misleading results.

Do not imply that a past appointment or prior testimony automatically qualifies a witness for every future matter.

In US federal practice, Rule 702 concerns the qualifications and reliability of expert testimony; the court makes admissibility determinations. A website cannot substitute for that process. See Federal Rule of Evidence 702.

6. An understandable professional trajectory

A career across universities, corporations, laboratories and independent practices can be difficult to read as a simple chronology.

A few tightly edited paragraphs can explain the progression without inventing a dramatic “founder story.” Distinguish historical roles from currently offered services.

7. Contact and engagement information

Make it easy for the appropriate professional to send a preliminary enquiry. A neutral description of availability, service geography and conflict-screening process may be useful when actually applicable.

Do not invite submission of privileged or sensitive case material through an unprotected form. A basic contact page should advise readers not to send confidential information until an engagement process is established.

What about LinkedIn and directories?

Keep them. A website can complement a law-firm page, institutional profile, professional directory and LinkedIn. Its added value is editorial coherence and a domain the expert controls—not artificially multiplying the same biography across the web.

Checklist

A one-minute checklist

  • The introduction explains actual areas of work.
  • Education, appointments and credentials are verified.
  • Specialties are useful to the intended evaluator.
  • Important publications or patents have source links.
  • Public testimony or project references meet confidentiality rules.
  • Historic roles are not mistaken for current affiliations.
  • Photos, quotes and third-party logos have appropriate permission.
  • The contact route is clear and doesn't request sensitive disclosures.
  • The site works on mobile and can be maintained.

Do all experts need their own website?

No. If current profiles already let counsel verify what matters, a new site may be unnecessary. If the record is dispersed, incomplete or difficult to navigate, a research-led professional platform can be useful.
