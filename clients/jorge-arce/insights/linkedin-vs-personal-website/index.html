LinkedIn vs. a Personal Website: What Established Professionals Actually Need

When does a senior consultant or expert need a website beyond LinkedIn? Compare what each does well and where each falls short—and decide what to build first.

A successful career leaves evidence everywhere: company biographies, talks, research databases, institutional pages, patents, interviews, articles and recommendations.

Yet the first thing someone finds about that career might be a short LinkedIn headline and a job history.

For a professional with a straightforward role and a reliable referral network, that may be enough. For someone whose work is complex, independent or spread across several decades, it may not be.

The question is not whether LinkedIn is good or bad. It is what someone needs to understand after they find your name.

What LinkedIn is very good at

LinkedIn is built for being discoverable within a professional network. It gives colleagues an easy way to find you, see where you have worked, confirm mutual connections and start a conversation. Your profile is also familiar to recruiters, collaborators and clients.

If most opportunities come from people who already understand your work, keeping a complete and current profile could be the most valuable thing you do.

A personal website is not a substitute for those relationships.

Where a LinkedIn profile can become limiting

Consider an independent polymer scientist whose career includes decades inside a large company, peer-reviewed papers, patents, technical advisory work and consulting assignments.

A prospective collaborator might need answers that a chronological list of roles does not make easy to find:

  • Which technical problems has this person worked on?
  • What is documented publicly, and where?
  • Which publications or patents are relevant to this question?
  • Are they working independently now?
  • What is the best route to contact them about a suitable project?

Or consider a former executive opening an advisory practice. Their corporate résumé proves where they worked, but does it explain what kind of advisory engagement they take now?

A website can make these answers clearer—not through more adjectives, but through better organization.

What an independent professional website can do

1. Put the work in one place. Build a concise overview, followed by selected areas of expertise, roles, publications, speaking engagements and references where appropriate. Link each externally verifiable item to its source.

2. Give the work context. A list of credentials tells someone what happened. Editorial structure helps them understand why those experiences are connected and where they are relevant today.

3. Separate your identity from one organization. Institutional pages and employer profiles are valuable references, but their purpose is to describe your role within that organization. An independent website can describe the longer arc.

4. Explain the next step. For a consultant: the types of engagements considered and how to enquire. For an academic: collaborations, speaking and public research. For a specialist: the right professional contact route.

5. Remain editable under your control. A domain owned by you gives you a durable home you can update as work and priorities change. It is still important to maintain it: ownership alone does not guarantee visibility.

A website is not a lead-generation strategy

This is the distinction we rarely see acknowledged in conversations about personal branding.

If you are struggling to generate demand, a beautiful site will not automatically create it. You may need sharper service positioning, warm introductions, partnerships, targeted outreach or better qualification.

A website becomes useful when it helps interested people evaluate you, whether they arrive from LinkedIn, a referral, a conference, a Google search or an email.

Think of LinkedIn as one potential route to your work. Think of your own site as a place where that work can be examined in more depth.

Should your site be long?

Usually, no. It should be as extensive as the reader's decision requires.

For some executives, an introduction, biography, selected work and contact page are enough. For a technical expert, searchable publications and source links may matter more than a large hero image. For a consultant, precise service descriptions and examples may be critical.

The strongest structure is often short at first glance, detailed where it matters.

Self-check

The seven-question professional record check

Open a private browser window and search your name as if you have never met yourself.

  1. Is it clear what you work on today, not just what titles you have held?
  2. Can someone identify three concrete areas of expertise?
  3. Can they follow links to verify at least the most important public claims?
  4. Is your work understandable without reading every line of a résumé?
  5. Does your professional identity make sense outside your current or former employer?
  6. Can the right person work out whether you accept relevant enquiries?
  7. Is there an easy, reliable way to reach you?

If several answers are no, start by improving your LinkedIn profile and the pages that already exist. Only build something new if there is a meaningful gap left to close.

Which should you invest in first?

SituationBetter first move
Your professional identity and work are simple, current and well representedImprove LinkedIn
Your profile is incomplete and references aren't linkedOrganize existing assets
Your career spans multiple institutions, publications and specialtiesConsider an independent professional site
You are transitioning into advisory workClarify your offer and buyer first, then decide how to present it
You need more opportunities but already have a credible siteFix acquisition/distribution, not necessarily the website

A different way to think about authority

Professional authority is earned through real work, not manufactured by a website.
