Adriana Mejía Cuartas

Coffee doesn’t need another hero. It needs better choreography.

Board Chair, Global Coffee Platform. Founder and CEO, Herencia VL.

The argument

Most projects in this sector do not fail for lack of money. They fail because someone treated an adaptive problem as a technical one.

A technical problem is solved with more resources. An adaptive problem is solved by changing the choreography between the people involved. Roughly eight in ten projects get that diagnosis wrong.

12.5 million

Coffee-growing families still living in poverty — after years of a growing conversation about sustainability and ESG. That gap between the conversation and the farm is the whole problem.

Four structural barriers

Not opinions about the sector. The four things that stop good intentions from reaching a farm.

01

Poverty forces a short horizon

Nobody invests on a five- or ten-year view without a stable income. The horizon a regenerative transition requires is the one poverty makes impossible.

02

Asymmetries of power and information

The producer does not know how the price of coffee is formed, or which climate risks are coming. Decisions are made where that information sits, and it does not sit on the farm.

03

Institutions that fail, or fail to coordinate

Several organisations arrive at the same farm with contradictory messages. The producer is left to arbitrate between them.

04

Women and young people left out of decisions

Cultural exclusion from the rooms where things are decided — which removes from the table exactly the people who will still be farming in twenty years.

How the work is done

Not a method with a name on it. A sequence, followed in order, that has produced programmes which outlast the person who designed them.

Diagnose the problem correctly

Is this solved with more resources, or does it require changing the choreography between the actors? Everything downstream depends on getting this one right.

Map the stakeholders and the power

Who decides. Who pays. Who implements. Who carries the risk. The asymmetries are identified before anything is designed, not after.

Design the what before the how

Picture the programme finished, ten years out, before starting it — and define success in the producer’s terms rather than the donor’s.

Build the financing architecture

Public capital, private capital, development cooperation and philanthropy, combined under a narrative umbrella wide enough to let the work scale.

Set the operating choreography

Shared definitions, shared measurement, shared investment, shared accountability. Projects require choreography, not command.

Regenerate the institution continuously

Programmes rarely fail at the start; they fail at the sustaining. The work is to build institutions that survive their leader.

Where I serve

Three ways of working, each with a different rhythm and a different room.

Boards

Board and governance service

Global boards in agriculture, food and retail, and multi-commodity groups whose supply-chain resilience and reputation are now a governance question rather than a communications one. Technical judgement in the room, from someone who has sat on both sides of the value chain.

Advisory

Strategic advisory and executive accompaniment

For organisations investing in sustainability without seeing it arrive at the farm, and for the executives leading them. Designed programme by programme, or as a standing quarterly engagement.

Speaking

Keynotes and moderation

Regeneration beyond the soil. Technical against adaptive. How more than a hundred and seventy stakeholders are moved as one. Most recently at EFICO’s centenary and at World of Coffee, both in Brussels in June 2026.

I serve on few boards at a time, and I am fully present on the ones I take.
