A title is easy to explain when a company gives it context.

“VP of Operations at Company X” tells colleagues where you fit. It does not necessarily tell a prospective independent client what problem you solve, how you work or why they should call you.

That distinction becomes important when you leave an established organization to advise others.

The first challenge is often not acquiring a website. It is translating a long career into **a clear offer that someone outside your network can understand**.

## Your credentials are the starting point, not the offer

You may have led large teams, built products, improved a manufacturing process or spent years working on a scientific problem.

Those experiences establish credibility. But a buyer starts somewhere else:

*What could this person help my organization do?*

A practical translation looks like this:

| Corporate language | Independent advisory language |
|---|---|
| “Former senior VP of Operations” | “I help multi-site operators diagnose delivery bottlenecks before expansion.” |
| “Thirty years in polymer R&D” | “I advise teams on technical questions in selected polymer applications.” |
| “Worked across multiple hotel properties” | “I support owners evaluating the repositioning of an existing property.” |

These are illustrative structures, not real biographies or assertions about any particular person. The second column must always be supported by what the professional can actually deliver.

## Step 1: Choose the buyer and the decision

“Businesses that need help” is not a target market.

Ask: who has the problem, which event makes it urgent, who authorizes the budget, and how do they currently solve it?

You may start with a narrow slice: owners considering a resort renovation, law firms evaluating a technical expert, or companies undertaking a specific operational transition.

Without that choice, neither your website nor your content knows what to communicate.

## Step 2: Make your evidence easy to evaluate

A senior CV is usually arranged as a chronology. A buyer often needs evidence organized by **problem and capability** instead.

Gather what you can document publicly and share with permission:

- work and engagements you can describe accurately;
- relevant positions, qualifications and appointments;
- selected public publications, patents or conference talks;
- your independent point of view on a challenge;
- references, where clients gave permission and claims are specific;
- clear boundaries: what you do not provide.

If your strongest work is confidential, do not invent case studies to fill the gap. Explain your methods, contexts and expertise at the appropriate level instead.

## Step 3: Put an intelligible offer in front of your history

Many consultant websites lead with a biography and leave the buyer to figure out services.

Reverse the order: first, who you help and with what kind of decision; next, evidence that you can help; then the longer history.

Consider a simple structure:
1. One-sentence explanation of what you advise on.
2. Three clearly defined problems or engagement types.
3. Relevant evidence and the professional trajectory behind it.
4. A simple, professional next step.

That next step does not need to be a complicated funnel. It can be a well-managed contact email.

## Step 4: Use the channels for different jobs

**LinkedIn** helps former colleagues, collaborators and introducers find and contact you.

**[An owned website](/insights/linkedin-vs-personal-website)** gives an interested third party a more structured, source-backed view of your practice.

**Introductions, partnerships and direct outreach** can generate early conversations while organic discovery builds slowly.

**Thoughtful publications** demonstrate judgment to readers, but only when grounded in real work and connected to a specific buyer question.

The channels reinforce one another. None alone replaces having a compelling offer.

## Step 5: Don't build too much before you talk to buyers

You do not need a 50-page website, a podcast, a paid community and a newsletter before sending your first thoughtful introduction.

Talk to potential clients. Learn their language. Identify what makes them say yes or no. Update how you present your expertise accordingly.

If you're receiving introductions but people struggle to understand your range of work, a better professional record may help. If nobody is considering you, first diagnose distribution and positioning.

## When an independent professional website is useful

A standalone site is especially useful when:
- your professional work spans several institutions or specialties;
- an evaluator needs to verify sources before deciding;
- you are no longer represented accurately by a single employer page;
- your services are distinct from your previous role;
- existing profiles scatter the evidence that matters most.

It may be unnecessary when your existing pages are accurate, clear and already serving the buyer.

::: panel Exercise
## A short exercise: the independent introduction

Finish these sentences in plain English:

- I work with **[specific buyer]**.
- They usually contact me when **[real trigger]**.
- The problems I can responsibly help with are **[two or three]**.
- My relevant background includes **[verifiable evidence]**.
- The next step is **[appropriate contact route]**.

If you cannot fill this in, start there—before commissioning any design.
